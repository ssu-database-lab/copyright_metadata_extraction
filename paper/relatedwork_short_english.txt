Related Work (short paragraphs) — English  [updated]
A NER–LLM Extractor for Metadata Extraction from
Korean Public-Domain Rights Documents
================================================================

[Notes]
· Sentences beginning with "this study/here" are suggestions; adjust to match
  the actual contributions. The six subheadings are organizational only.


[1] BIO Tagging and BIO-based Sequence-labeling NER

Ramshaw & Marcus (1995) recast chunking as a sequence-labeling problem by
assigning B (beginning), I (inside), or O (outside) tags to each token,
introducing the IOB (BIO) scheme now widely used in NER. The NER stage of this
study also adopts this BIO scheme.

Huang et al. (2015) show that a BiLSTM with a CRF layer (BiLSTM-CRF) jointly
exploits both left/right context and the sequential dependency among token
labels, establishing the starting point for neural sequence-labeling NER.

Lample et al. (2016) achieve state-of-the-art results on CoNLL-2003 with a
BiLSTM-CRF that combines pretrained word and character embeddings, forming the
standard NER lineage that later extends to pretrained-transformer token
classification. This study's KLUE-BERT-based BIO token classification builds on
that lineage.


[2] LLM-based NER and Information Extraction

GPT-NER (Wang, S. et al., 2023) introduces a self-verification strategy in
which the LLM re-checks its own outputs, achieving performance comparable to
fully supervised models on five standard datasets and significantly surpassing
supervised models in low-resource settings. However, it has the LLM perform
NER directly in English, general-domain text, and does not examine a
configuration that divides the work between a dedicated NER model and an LLM.

InstructUIE (Wang, X. et al., 2023) models NER, relation extraction, and event
extraction in a unified instruction-based text-to-text format and proposes a
benchmark of 32 IE datasets. It is on par with BERT-based models under
supervision and substantially outperforms GPT-3.5 in the zero-shot setting,
but, being general-domain, it does not address domain-specific entities such
as the rightsholders and usage conditions found in public-domain rights
documents.

UniversalNER (Zhou et al., 2024) distills ChatGPT's NER ability into smaller
models that, without any direct supervision, far surpass general
instruction-tuned models across 43 datasets in 9 domains. Its strength,
however, lies in arbitrary open entity types; predefined general categories
alone cannot capture the fine-grained rights and license entities required
here.


[3] Using the LLM as a Verification/Reranking Step
    (the direct basis for the proposed pipeline)

Ma et al. (2023) report that, in most settings, recent LLMs are inferior to
fine-tuned small language models (SLMs) in accuracy, latency, and cost. Yet
they show that, with appropriate prompting, an LLM can complement an SLM on
hard samples, and they propose a filter-then-rerank paradigm in which the SLM
screens samples and the LLM re-ranks the difficult ones.

VerifiNER (Kim, S. et al., 2024) presents a verification-augmented NER
framework that uses the LLM's knowledge-grounded reasoning to verify and
refine the outputs of an existing NER model. It is not, however, specialized
for the rights-document domain or for Korean rights entities.

NESTLE (Cho et al., 2024) combines a commercial LLM with an internal
small-scale IE model and attains GPT-4-comparable performance on Korean
precedent information extraction using only 4 human-labeled and 192
LLM-labeled examples. Its target, however, is statistical analysis of court
precedents, and it does not address extraction of public-domain rights/license
metadata or normalization into a rights-expression schema.


[4] Difficulty of NER in Legal/Specialized Domains and Domain Adaptation

CUAD (Hendrycks et al., 2021) is an expert-annotated contract-review dataset
(510 contracts, 41 clause categories, 13,101 labels) showing that Transformer
performance depends heavily on model design and data scale. It is, however,
limited to clause-level review of English contracts and does not cover the
rights relations and usage conditions of Korean public-domain works.

E-NER (Au et al., 2022), a legal NER corpus built from U.S. SEC EDGAR filings,
finds that applying a model trained on general English (CoNLL-2003) to legal
text drops F1 by 29.4–60.4%. This demonstrates that naively applying general
NER models is inadequate, but it targets English SEC filings and does not
address domain adaptation for Korean rights documents.

Skylaki et al. (2020) propose a text-to-text NER approach that, instead of
token labeling, uses a pointer-generator network to directly generate entities
from noisy U.S. court text containing PDF-extraction and OCR errors and
incomplete labels. It targets English court cases, however, and does not
combine LLM verification or a rights-expression schema.

Yang & Agrawal (2023) combine document layout analysis with weak supervision
to extract complex named entities from legal documents, showing that a model
trained only on pseudo-labels outperforms the supervised baseline when gold
data is scarce. It targets English legal documents, however, and does not
address Korean rights documents or rights-expression normalization.


[5] Joint NER+RE and Structured Output

Zhong & Chen (2021) (PURE) show that a simple pipeline—recognizing entities
first and then feeding their types as markers into a relation model—outperforms
joint models by 1.7–2.8% relation F1 on ACE04, ACE05, and SciERC, and find that
entity and relation representations capture distinct information so that sharing
them hurts performance. This supports the present study's staged separation of
NER and LLM verification.

Dunn et al. (2022) fine-tune a pretrained GPT-3 on roughly 500 prompt–
completion pairs to perform NER and relation extraction jointly and output
results in structured formats such as JSON objects. It targets the
materials-science domain, however, and does not address normalization into a
rights-metadata schema.


[6] Korean Resources and LLM-assisted Labeling

KLUE (Park et al., 2021) is a standard Korean NLU benchmark that includes NER
and relation extraction and provides baseline performance for the Korean
pretrained models KLUE-BERT/RoBERTa. As a general-domain benchmark, however,
it does not sufficiently cover the copyright- and rights-specific entities of
public-domain works.

KBMC (Byun et al., 2024) builds a Korean medical NER dataset with ChatGPT
assistance, improving medical NER performance by about 20% over models trained
on general Korean NER data. Although a medical-domain study, it suggests—being
a low-resource Korean specialized domain—that LLM-assisted labeling is also
viable for rights documents.

Song (2024) uses BERT-based NER to extract 173 person names and 314
organization names from 1960s–70s handwritten Guro Industrial Complex records
(~1,200 pages) for use as metadata and search indexes. As the closest domestic
precedent for extracting metadata from Korean documents via NER, it nonetheless
targets archival records and does not address copyright or rights entities.


[7] Rights-Expression Schemas and LLM-based Rights Extraction

The ODRL Information Model 2.2 (W3C, 2018) is a policy language for
machine-interpretable expression of permissions, prohibitions, and duties over
use, defining concepts such as Policy, Permission, Prohibition, Duty, Asset,
Party, and Constraint. As an expression vocabulary, however, it does not
address extraction from unstructured documents; this study uses it as the
target schema for normalization.

ccREL (Abelson et al., 2008) is an RDF-based rights-expression scheme for
copyright licenses that links works, licenses, and permitted/prohibited/
required actions via properties such as cc:permits, cc:prohibits, cc:requires,
and cc:attributionName. As another standard vocabulary, it serves here as a
reference for designing a metadata schema suited to rights documents.

The Korea Open Government License (KOGL; Korea Copyright Commission) is the
standard licensing-mark system that lets the public freely use public works
under type-specific conditions without individual permission, specifying
obligations such as attribution and prohibitions such as non-commercial use and
no modification. Not a research paper, it is the concrete definition of the
usage conditions/prohibitions/duties this study extracts and normalizes, and is
cited here as a domain primary source.

Mustafa et al. (2024) have GPT-4 automatically generate ODRL policies from
natural-language instructions, using the ODRL ontology and its documentation
as the prompt and applying self-correction rules for refinement. This
addresses generating policies from clean instructions, however, not extraction
from noisy real-world rights documents.

The Pr²Graph framework (2025, arXiv:2509.01716) uses an LLM to extract key
rights/practice information from privacy policies, builds a knowledge graph,
and converts it into formal policy representations such as ODRL or psDToU. It
is the closest precedent for this study's "unstructured rights document → LLM
extraction → ODRL normalization" pipeline, though its domain is privacy
policies rather than copyright rights documents.
